01 / GENERATIONTHE SEAFLOOR MOVES

First, the ocean floor shifts.

A large undersea earthquake can abruptly lift or lower part of the seafloor, displacing the water above it. That disturbance starts a long wave. Undersea landslides and volcanic activity can also generate tsunamis. The trigger and the shape of the seafloor movement matter.

02 / PROPAGATIONENERGY CROSSES THE BASIN

Across deep water, it is a long wave.

A tsunami moves through the full depth of the ocean, not only at the surface. In deep water, its wave height may be small and hard to notice, even while the wave travels quickly across an ocean basin. INCOIS describes open-ocean speeds of roughly 500–1,000 km/h.

03 / SHOALING & RUN-UPTHE SHORELINE CHANGES THE WAVE

Near shore, depth changes the story.

As a tsunami reaches shallower water, it slows and its wavelength shortens. The water level and currents can build into a powerful surge. Bays, inlets, reefs, and coastal shape influence how the wave behaves and where water travels inland.

This nearshore transformation is called shoaling; the inland reach is described by inundation and run-up.

04 / DETECTION & RESPONSEOBSERVATION BECOMES AN ADVISORY

Science helps turn signals into time.

India’s Tsunami Early Warning Centre at INCOIS combines earthquake information, deep-ocean and coastal sea-level observations, and model scenarios. A 24×7 centre assesses the threat and disseminates advisories to authorities. A warning system helps—but communities still need clear routes and practiced plans.
