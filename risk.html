01 / A practical lens

Four questions help us understand risk.

Risk assessment looks beyond the hazard itself. It asks what could happen, who and what is in its path, and what strengths can help limit harm.

01 — HAZARD

What could happen?

The potential tsunami and its characteristics, including its source and how it may travel toward a coast.

02 — EXPOSURE

Who and what is there?

People, homes, roads, services, and livelihoods located in areas that could be affected.

03 — VULNERABILITY

What makes harm more likely?

Physical, social, economic, and environmental conditions that can increase susceptibility to impacts.

04 — CAPACITY

What helps people respond?

Knowledge, resources, trusted alerts, evacuation routes, and community plans that support action.

Keep in mind: this framework helps organize questions. It is not a numerical risk score, and it cannot replace local hazard maps or official advice.
02 / India's Indian Ocean setting

Distance changes the time to respond.

INCOIS monitors tsunami-generating source regions and combines earthquake information, ocean observations, and model scenarios to assess threats to Indian coasts.

SOURCE REGIONS

More than one direction matters.

03 / WHAT THE RECORD SHOWS

One event. A wide footprint.

A Government of India report published in 2005 estimated that 27.92 lakh people were affected across 1,089 villages in the impacted states and union territories. The event demonstrated that tsunami impacts can reach island communities and mainland coasts—and disrupt homes, services, and livelihoods.

04 / What shapes vulnerability?

Look at the coast—and the community.

These are factors an assessment should investigate. Their importance varies from place to place.

FACTOR 01

Elevation & coastal shape

Local topography and bathymetry influence how tsunami water moves and where inundation may occur.

FACTOR 02

People & essential services

Homes, schools, roads, ports, health services, and livelihoods within mapped hazard zones contribute to exposure.

FACTOR 03

Routes, access & warnings

Clear evacuation maps, accessible alerts, safe destinations, and practiced plans can strengthen response capacity.

05 / From observation to action

A warning matters when people can act on it.

India's early warning system combines monitoring and modelling. Local authorities guide protective action for their communities.

NATURAL WARNING SIGNS

Know what the ocean may tell you.

Near the coast, natural signs may be your first warning. Do not wait for an official message if you experience them.

Strong or long earthquake shaking
A loud roar from the ocean
Unusual sea-level change or sudden retreat

NEXT / COMMUNITY READINESS

Preparedness is built together.

Hazard maps, evacuation routes, public information, reliable alert channels, and community practice all help turn risk knowledge into readiness. “Tsunami Ready” recognition reflects preparedness measures; it does not mean a community is tsunami-proof.
